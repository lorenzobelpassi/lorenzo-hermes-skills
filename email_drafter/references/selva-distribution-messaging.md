# Selva Distribution Messaging: Stable Sourcing Rhythm + Trust

Use this when drafting Selva Partners/Cosecha messaging for chefs, owners, F&B directors, hotel buyers, procurement, LinkedIn posts, onboarding copy, or case studies around the distribution model.

## Core doctrine

Selva is not a broadline distributor and should not sound like one.

Position as:
- disciplined specialty procurement desk for chef-led accounts
- demand-aggregated sourcing platform for premium kitchens
- controlled access to better product, with adult economics behind it

Best line:
> Access follows discipline.

## Stable house rhythm — not custom cadence drift

User correction: do **not** over-adapt cadence to every chef. Selva should set the sourcing rhythm and train chefs to adhere to it. Metrics refine Selva's house rhythm internally; they do not let every account create its own rhythm.

Say:
- “We work through structured sourcing windows.”
- “Tuesday is when we close the allocation for this drop.”
- “If you want access to the better allocations, we need you inside the sourcing window.”
- “This is the cycle that gives you the best product.”

Avoid:
- “We build the ordering rhythm around your kitchen.”
- “What day works best for you?”
- “We can try to make it work.”

Customer-facing explanation:
> Selva works through set sourcing windows, committed orders, and planned drops. That lets us buy against real demand, reduce waste, protect product quality, and keep delivery economics stable.

## Aggregated demand model

Once multiple restaurants adhere to Selva's rhythm, Selva becomes an aggregated specialty buying desk.

Flywheel:
1. Selva publishes sourcing/allocation window.
2. Chefs submit needs, budgets, use case, timing, and flexibility.
3. Selva aggregates demand across accounts.
4. Selva shops/sources/negotiates.
5. Selva matches product to budget within reason.
6. Chefs trust the rhythm.
7. More demand improves buying power, route density, supplier attention, and product optionality.

Do not frame as “we aggregate demand to improve our margin.” Frame as:
> When chefs order inside our sourcing window, we can combine demand across accounts and go back to the market with more leverage. That gives us a better chance of finding the right product at the right price while protecting quality.

## Budget matching without becoming a price-matching servant

Promise:
- disciplined sourcing
- honest market read
- best available fit for budget
- better access through aggregated demand
- clear tradeoffs

Do not promise:
- cheapest price
- exact match every time
- unlimited last-minute sourcing
- broadline-style terms
- blind price matching

Tradeoff language:
> I can protect budget if you give me flexibility. I can protect specificity if you give me time. I can protect timing if you give me price flexibility.

Ask chefs for:
- target budget
- use case/dish
- volume
- quality threshold
- flexibility on variety/origin/size
- delivery date
- substitution tolerance

## Local-first sourcing ladder

Important distinction: Selva is local-first, not local-blind.

Best line:
> Local is the preference. Performance is the standard. Continuity is the system.

For key categories, map:
- Primary: local source when it performs
- Secondary: regional/domestic continuity option
- Tertiary: specialty/import option when specificity requires it

Customer-facing explanation:
> We prefer local when it performs, but we do not hide behind local when the crop is weak. For key items, we map secondary and tertiary sources so chefs have continuity without blind substitutions.

LinkedIn angle:
> Local-first is not enough. Crops gap. Weather hits. Volume disappears. A serious sourcing program needs a ladder: local when it performs, regional when continuity matters, import when specificity requires it.

## Case study pattern

Use anonymized proof, not sensitive account details.

Structure:
1. Problem: reactive buying, crop gap, quality inconsistency, budget pressure, waste risk.
2. Selva approach: structured sourcing cycle, committed allocation, aggregated demand, sourcing ladder, buy/watch/skip guidance.
3. Result: cleaner process, fewer blind substitutions, more continuity, better budget fit, lower waste risk.
4. Lesson: specialty produce should be planned through a disciplined rhythm, not bought like commodity product.

## LinkedIn cadence and tone

Target: chefs, owners/operators, F&B directors, hotel buyers, procurement, cruise/hospitality decision makers.

Tone:
- direct, opinionated, useful
- premium but not precious
- insider, not influencer
- no founder fluff unless tied to a sharp operational lesson

Content pillars:
- product intelligence
- operator economics
- Selva philosophy
- case studies
- behind-the-desk sourcing process

Sample post hook:
> Specialty produce does not work on emergency timing.

Sample body:
> The best product has a rhythm: market read, allocation window, committed orders, planned sourcing, clean delivery. The kitchens that get the best product are usually not the ones ordering last-minute. They are the ones who know how to buy inside the window.
